\noindent\textbf{Problem 1:}
\medskip

Compute the determinants of the following matrices. \emph{Note:} See Appendix B if needed for a refresher on complex numbers.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item $\begin{bmatrix}
            8 & 0  \\
            3 & -1
          \end{bmatrix}$
  \item $\begin{bmatrix}
            2i & 3  \\
            4  & 6i
          \end{bmatrix}$, where $i = \sqrt{-1}$
  \item $\begin{bmatrix}
            i  & 2 + i & 0   \\
            -1 & 3     & 2i  \\
            0  & -1    & 1-i
          \end{bmatrix}$, where $i = \sqrt{-1}$
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Answer:}

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item The definition of the determinant of a $2 \times 2$ matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix}
            a & b \\
            c & d
          \end{bmatrix}$ is $\det A = ad - bc$.

        Here then we can calculate: \begin{align*}
          \det \begin{bmatrix}
                 8 & 0  \\
                 3 & -1
               \end{bmatrix} & = (8)(-1) - (0)(3) \\[1ex]
                                 & = -8 - 0       \\
                                 & = -8
        \end{align*}
  \item This case is the same as the one above, except that we must perform some multiplication of complex numbers. \begin{align*}
          \det \begin{bmatrix}
                 2i & 3  \\
                 4  & 6i
               \end{bmatrix} & = (2i)(6i) - (3)(4) \\[1ex]
                                 & = 12i^2 - 12    \\[1ex]
                                 & = 12(-1) - 12   \\[1ex]
                                 & = -12 - 12      \\[1ex]
                                 & = -24           \\[1ex]
        \end{align*}
  \item In this third case, we have to take advantage of cofactor expansion. (I have added braces to make the algebra easier to trace visually.)
        \begin{align*}
          \det A & = \det \begin{bmatrix}
                            i  & 2 + i & 0   \\
                            -1 & 3     & 2i  \\
                            0  & -1    & 1-i
                          \end{bmatrix}  \reason{Given}                                                                                                                                                        \\[1ex]
                 & = \underbrace{a_{11}C_{11}} + \underbrace{a_{12}C_{12}} + \underbrace{a_{13}C_{13}} \reason{By definition of determinant \cite[Ch. 3.1, p.173]{LayEtAl2020LinearAlgebraAndItsApplications}} \\[1ex]
                 & = \underbrace{iC_{11}} + \underbrace{(2 + i)C_{12}} + \underbrace{(0)C_{13}} \reason{By substitution}                                                                                       \\[1ex]
                 & = \underbrace{i(-1)^{1+1}\det A_{11}} + \underbrace{(2 + i)(-1)^{1+2}\det A_{12}} \reason{By substitution and simplification of mult. by 0}                                                 \\[1ex]
                 & = \underbrace{i(-1)^{2}\det A_{11}} + \underbrace{(2 + i)(-1)^{3}\det A_{12}} \reason{By simplification}                                                                                    \\[1ex]
                 & = \underbrace{i\det A_{11}} + \underbrace{(2 + i)(-1)\det A_{12}} \reason{By simplification}                                                                                                \\[1ex]
                 & = \underbrace{i\det \begin{bmatrix}3 & 2i \\-1 & 1-i\end{bmatrix}} + \underbrace{(2 + i)(-1)\det \begin{bmatrix}-1 & 2i \\0 & 1-i\end{bmatrix}} \reason{By simplification}                  \\[1ex]
                 & = \underbrace{i(3(1-i) - (-1(2i)))} + \underbrace{(2 + i)(-1)(-1(1-i)-2i(0))} \reason{By evaluation of the determinants}                                                                    \\[1ex]
                 & = \underbrace{i(3 - 3i + 2i)} + \underbrace{(2 + i)(-1)(-1 + i)} \reason{By simplification}                                                                                                 \\[1ex]
                 & = \underbrace{i(3 - i)} + \underbrace{(2 + i)(1 - i)} \reason{By simplification}                                                                                                              \\[1ex]
                 & = \underbrace{3i - i^2} + \underbrace{2 - 2i + i - i^2} \reason{By multiplication}                                                                                                          \\[1ex]
                 & = \underbrace{3i - (-1)} + \underbrace{2 - i - (-1)} \reason{By evaluation of $i^2$}                                                                                                        \\[1ex]
                 & = \underbrace{3i + 1} + \underbrace{2 - i + 1} \reason{By multiplication by $-1$}                                                                                                           \\[1ex]
                 & = 2i + 4 \reason{By addition}                                                                                                                                                               \\[1ex]
                 & = 2(2 + i) \reason{By factoring, commutativity}
        \end{align*}
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Problem 2:}
\medskip

(Continuation of the prior assignment.) Let the matrices $A = \begin{bmatrix}
    3  & -2 & -2 \\
    -1 & 1  & 1  \\
    3  & -1 & -2
  \end{bmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{bmatrix}
    1  & a & 0  \\
    -1 & b & 1  \\
    2  & c & -1
  \end{bmatrix}$ be inverses of each other. Find the value of $b$ using Cramer's Rule. Compare your answer to your prior assignment.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Answer:}

In the previous assignment, we took advantage of the argument in \textcite[Ch. 2.2, p.~117]{LayEtAl2020LinearAlgebraAndItsApplications}, which says that row reduction $r: A \to I$ can be viewed as concurrently solving the equation $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ for multiple values of $\vec{b}$, where the value of each $\vec{b}$ corresponds to a column of the identity matrix $I$. When we used that method, we found the solution to $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{e}_2$ is $\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix}
    2 \\0\\3
  \end{bmatrix}$, and consequently $b = 0$.

Using Cramer's rule will give us a formula for $b$ directly, though it will require calculating several determinants. In Cramer's rule, where the matrix $A$ is an $n \times n$ matrix, \begin{align*}
  x_i & = \frac{\det A_i(\vec{b})}{\det A},\text{ for } i = 1,2,\dots n
\end{align*}

Applying Cramer's rule to find the value of $b$, we need to replace an appropriate column of $A$ with a corresponding column from the identity matrix $I$. Since we want to find $b$, which is in position $B_{2,2}$, we need to set $\vec{b}$ to the second column of $I$ and replace the second column of $A$ in Cramer's rule. Concretely,

\begin{align*}
  x_2 = b & = \frac{\det A_2(\vec{b})}{\det A} \reason{where $\vec{b} = \begin{bmatrix}
                                                     0 \\ 1 \\ 0
                                                   \end{bmatrix}$}
\end{align*}

\noindent Let's first calculate $\det A$, the denominator.

\begin{align*}
  \det A & = \det \begin{bmatrix}
                    3  & -2 & -2 \\
                    -1 & 1  & 1  \\
                    3  & -1 & -2
                  \end{bmatrix}                                                                                                                                                                                                                       \\[1ex]
         & = \underbrace{a_{11}C_{11}} + \underbrace{a_{12}C_{12}} + \underbrace{a_{13}C_{13}} \reason{By def of determinant \cite[Ch. 3.1, p.173]{LayEtAl2020LinearAlgebraAndItsApplications}}                                                          \\[1ex]
         & = \underbrace{3(-1)^2\begin{vmatrix}1 & 1 \\ -1 & -2\end{vmatrix}} + \underbrace{-2 (-1)^3 \begin{vmatrix}-1 & 1 \\ 3 & -2\end{vmatrix}} + \underbrace{-2 (-1)^4 \begin{vmatrix}-1 & 1 \\ 3 & -1\end{vmatrix}} \reason{By cofactor expansion} \\[1ex]
         & = \underbrace{3(1)(-2 - (-1))} + \underbrace{-2 (-1) (2 - 3) } + \underbrace{-2 (1) (1 - 3) } \reason{By evaluation of determinants}                                                                                                          \\[1ex]
         & = \underbrace{3(-1)} + \underbrace{-2} + \underbrace{4} \reason{By simplification}                                                                                                                                                            \\[1ex]
         & = -3 -2 + 4 \reason{By simplification}                                                                                                                                                                                                        \\[1ex]
         & = -1 \reason{By addition}                                                                                                                                                                                                                     \\[1ex]
\end{align*}

\noindent Now we can calculate the numerator,

\begin{align*}
  \det A_2(\vec{b}) & = \begin{vmatrix}
                          3  & 0 & -2 \\
                          -1 & 1 & 1  \\
                          3  & 0 & -2
                        \end{vmatrix} \reason{By definition of $A_{i}(\vec{b})$ and since $\vec{b} = \vec{e_2}$} \\[1ex]
\end{align*}

Fortunately for us, we do not need to do cofactor expansion to evaluate this determinant. Note that in $A_2(\vec{b})$, $R_1 = R_3$. Hence, the rows are linearly dependent, and $\det A_2(\vec{b}) = 0$ by the Invertible Matrix Theorem (or alternatively by row reduction and then \cite[Theorem 3.2, p. 175]{LayEtAl2020LinearAlgebraAndItsApplications} that says that in a triangular matrix, the determinant is the product of the diagonal entries, since here here the diagonal entries will be [3,1,0]). This provides us with what we need to find the value of $b$.

\begin{align*}
  x_2 = b & = \frac{\det A_2(\vec{b})}{\det A} \reason{By Cramer's Rule} \\[1ex]
          & = \frac{0}{-1} \reason{By substitution}                      \\
          & = 0 \reason{By division}
\end{align*}

This corresponds to the solution that we found previously by the row reduction method in module 5.

\noindent\textbf{Problem 3:}
\medskip

Let $T: R^3 \to R^3$ be the linear transformation determined by the matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix}
    a & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & b & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & c
  \end{bmatrix}$, where $a$, $b$, and $c$ are positive real numbers. Let $B$ be the unit sphere defined by $x^{2}_{1} + x^{2}_{2} + x^{2}_{3} = 1$

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item Show that the image $T(B)$ is the ellipsoid with the equation $\frac{x^{2}_{1}}{a^2} + \frac{x^{2}_{2}}{b^2} + \frac{x^{2}_{3}}{c^2} = 1$
  \item Use determinants and the fact that the volume of the unit sphere is $\frac{4}{3}\pi$ to determine the volume formula for the ellipsoid in part (a).
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Answer:}

This holds for similar reasons to those in \cite[Example 5, Ch. 3.3, p. 195]{LayEtAl2020LinearAlgebraAndItsApplications} that deals with an ellipse's being the image of the unit disk under the transformation $T$ whose corresponding matrix is $A_{\text{Ex}5} = \begin{bmatrix}
    a & 0 \\
    0 & b
  \end{bmatrix}$. There, the unit disk $D$ consists of the region bounded by the unit circle. The unit circle is described by $u_1^2 + u_2^2 = 1$. Hence, the unit disk, which is the region bounded by the unit circle, is described by $u_1^2 + u_2^2 \le 1$. See Figure \ref{fig:unit-disk}.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \UnitDiskFig[a=1, b=1, a label={$1$}, b label={$1$}, fill=blue!25, x1 label=$u_1$, x2 label=$u_2$, label below=$D$, y extent=1.6, scale=1]
  \caption{Unit Disk}\label{fig:unit-disk}
\end{figure}

\footnote{To create these diagrams, I had Claude \cite*{Anthropic2026ClaudeOpus55} generate parameterized macros using TikZ, which can be found in the source code repository I keep for this class here \url{https://github.com/jllovet/jhu-en-625-252-fa26-linear-algebra}.}

It's worth spelling out some of the argument from \cite[Example 5, Ch. 3.3, p. 195]{LayEtAl2020LinearAlgebraAndItsApplications} more explicitly than the book's treatment of it, so that we can see more clearly how to generalize to the sphere $\to$ ellipsoid case.

Each point in the unit disk is described by a vector $\vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix}u_1\\u_2\end{bmatrix}$. Each corresponding point of the region bound by the ellipse is described by $A\vec{u} = \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{bmatrix}$.

Computing the matrix-vector product $A\vec{u}$ explicitly.

\begin{align*}
  A\vec{u} & = \begin{bmatrix}a & 0\\0 & b\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u_1 \\ u_2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}au_1 + 0u_2\\0u_1 + bu_2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}au_1\\bu_2\end{bmatrix} \\
\end{align*}

But then, by the equation $A\vec{u} = \vec{x}$,

\begin{align*}
  \begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{bmatrix} & = \begin{bmatrix}au_1\\bu_2\end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}

Therefore, we can calculate componentwise:
\begin{align*}
  x_1 = au_1 & \iff \frac{x_1}{a} = u_1 \\
  x_2 = bu_2 & \iff \frac{x_2}{b} = u_2
\end{align*}

The preceding shows that the ellipse $E$ is the result of applying the linear transformation $T$ that corresponds to the matrix $A$ to the unit disk $D$. This is illustrated by Figure \ref*{fig:unit-disk-to-ellipse} below.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \UnitDiskFig[a=1, b=1, a label={$1$}, b label={$1$}, fill=blue!25, x1 label=$u_1$, x2 label=$u_2$,
    label below=$D$, y extent=1.6, scale=.75]
  \quad $\xrightarrow{\;T\;}$ \quad
  \EllipseFig[a=2, b=.75, a label=$a$, b label=$b$, fill=blue!25, x1 label=$x_1$, x2 label=$x_2$,
    label below=$E$, y extent=1.6, scale=.75]
  \caption{$(T: D \to E) \leadsto (\vec{x} = A\vec{u})$}\label{fig:unit-disk-to-ellipse}
\end{figure}

This amounts to a dilation of space by $a$ along $u_1$ to $x_1$ and by $b$ along $u_2$ to $x_2$. Hence, a vector is in $D$ if and only if that same vector is in $E$ after applying the transformation $T$. That is, $u \in D \iff T(u) \in E$, or equivalently
\begin{align*}
  u_1^2 + u_2^2 \le 1 \iff \left(\frac{x_1}{a}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{x_2}{b}\right)^2 \le 1
\end{align*}

From here it is straightforward to extend to the case of $R^3$, where we are considering the unit sphere and an ellipsoid.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \UnitSphereFig[a=1, b=1, a label={$1$}, b label={$1$}, c label={$1$}, fill=blue!25, x1 label=$u_1$, x2 label=$u_2$, x3 label=$u_3$, label below=$B$, y extent=1.6, scale=.75]
  \quad $\xrightarrow{\;T\;}$ \quad
  \EllipsoidFig[a=1.2, b=1.6, c=1, a label=$a$, b label=$b$, c label=$c$, fill=blue!25, equator=true, label below={$T(B)$}, scale=1]
  \caption{$(T: B \to T(B)) \leadsto (\vec{x} = A\vec{u})$}\label{fig:unit-sphere-to-ellipsoid}
\end{figure}

\begin{align*}
  A\vec{u} & = \begin{bmatrix}
                 a & 0 & 0 \\
                 0 & b & 0 \\
                 0 & 0 & c
               \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u_1 \\ u_2\\u_3\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}au_1 + 0u_2 + 0u_3\\0u_1 + bu_2 + 0u_3\\0u_1 + 0u_2 + cu_3\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}au_1\\bu_2\\cu_3\end{bmatrix} \\
\end{align*}

As before, consider the equation $A\vec{u} = \vec{x}$, here for $A$ and $\vec{u}$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

\begin{align*}
  \begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\x_3\end{bmatrix} & = \begin{bmatrix}au_1\\bu_2\\cu_3\end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}

Then we have another similar componentwise calculation:
\begin{align*}
  x_1 = au_1 & \iff \frac{x_1}{a} = u_1 \\
  x_2 = bu_2 & \iff \frac{x_2}{b} = u_2 \\
  x_3 = cu_3 & \iff \frac{x_3}{c} = u_3
\end{align*}

This represents a similar dilation of space along each of the three components of $\mathbb{R}^3$ as we saw before with $\mathbb{R}^2$. Hence, a vector is in $B$ if and only if that same vector is in $T(B)$ after applying the transformation $T$ to $B$. That is, $u \in B \iff T(u) \in T(B)$, or equivalently
\begin{align*}
  u_1^2 + u_2^2 + u_3^2 \le 1 \iff \left(\frac{x_1}{a}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{x_2}{b}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{x_3}{c}\right)^2 \le 1
\end{align*}

Therefore, the region described by $T(B)$ is circumscribed by the ellipsoid whose equation is $\frac{x_1^2}{a^2} + \frac{x_2^2}{b^2} + \frac{x_3^2}{c^2} = 1$, and this is the image of $B$ under $T$, which what what we wanted.

In light of this, let's find the volume formula for the ellipsoid $T(B)$. Two observations will help here. First, the matrix $A$ is a triangular matrix. By \cite[Theorem 3.2, Ch. 3.1, p. 175]{LayEtAl2020LinearAlgebraAndItsApplications}, the determinant of each is the product of the elements along its diagonal.

\begin{align*}
  \det A & = \begin{vmatrix}
               a & 0 & 0 \\
               0 & b & 0 \\
               0 & 0 & c
             \end{vmatrix} = a \cdot b \cdot c = abc \\[1ex]
\end{align*}

Second, by \cite[Theorem 3.10, Ch. 3.3, p. 193]{LayEtAl2020LinearAlgebraAndItsApplications} and the extension thereof \cite[Ch. 3.3, p.195]{LayEtAl2020LinearAlgebraAndItsApplications}, because a unit sphere $B$ bounds a finite region in $\mathbb{R}^3$ and the ellipsoid $T(B)$ similarly bounds a finite region in $\mathbb{R}^3$, where $T$ is defined by the matrix $A$, therefore the volume of $T(B)$ is equal to $\mid \det A \mid \cdot \text{ volume of B}$. Let $V(a)$ denote the volume of an object $a$. Then,

\begin{align*}
  V(T(B)) & = \mid \det A \mid \cdot V(B) \reason{By \cite[Theorem 3.10, Ch. 3.3, p. 193]{LayEtAl2020LinearAlgebraAndItsApplications}} \\[1ex]
               & = \mid \det \mid A \cdot \frac{4}{3}\pi \reason{By volume of sphere}                                                       \\[1ex]
               & = \mid abc \mid \cdot \frac{4}{3}\pi \reason{By substitution}                                                              \\[1ex]
\end{align*}

Therefore, the volume of the ellipsoid is given by the formula $v = \mid abc \mid \cdot \frac{4}{3}\pi$.
